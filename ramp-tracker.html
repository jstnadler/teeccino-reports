📖 Metric Index — every metric on this page, defined

Cost & value of a customer

nCPA (cost per new customer)
What we pay in ads to win one new customer: total ad spend ÷ new customers. The headline light tracks this vs what a customer is worth.
A new customer is worth ($50)
Blended 2-year contribution margin per new customer — the break-even line. Below it, acquisition adds profit.
A subscriber is worth ($118)
2-year CM when the first order is a subscription (~2.4× the blend) — 5.4 orders / 81% repeat. Shows the prize for converting first orders to subscription.
First-order break-even ($18)
CM on a new customer's first order alone. nCPA below this = cash-positive on order one; above it, you recoup over the repeat tail.
Net revenue
subtotal − discounts (excludes shipping & tax) — the realized sales value the customer paid for product. The base of CM and projected revenue below.
COGS
Cost of goods sold — the product cost per order from the COGS table.
Shipping cost
Actual per-order carrier cost (ShipStation) for 2024-onward orders; a ~$7.50 estimate for the small pre-2024 tail.
Contribution margin (CM)
Net revenue − COGS − shipping cost — the same lean definition as the Monthly KPI. Ad spend is the separate CAC, not subtracted here.
DTC
Direct-to-consumer — teeccino.com orders only. Wholesale (Walmart, Target, Faire) is excluded everywhere on this page.

Payback & efficiency

Payback
Months until a new customer's cumulative CM recoups the nCPA we paid, read off the LTV→CM curve. Past the $50 2-yr ceiling it never pays back.
Profit / order
Lean CM per order after product, shipping & ad cost — the weekly per-order contribution.
MER
Marketing Efficiency Ratio = projected net revenue ÷ ad spend. A sanity check, not a target.
Ad % of revenue (TACOS)
Ad spend as a percent of revenue. Lower = more efficient; it's just MER inverted. Trailing-12-mo actual is in the stat at top.
HEALTHY / WATCH / ALERT
The gate light: nCPA under $50 = healthy, $50–$56 = watch, above $56 = pull back.

What this shows
What this shows. Read it as: "if a customer cost me $X to acquire, how long until they've earned that $X back in profit?" The X-axis is your acquisition cost (nCPA); the Y-axis is months until that customer's cumulative contribution margin climbs back up to what you paid. The marker is where your current cost sits.

Where the curve comes from. A customer's contribution margin piles up fast early, then flattens as their value reaches ~$50 blended lifetime CM (mature 2024-and-earlier cohorts, contribution margin):
ByCumulative CM
~30 days$21
~90 days$25
1 year$39
Lifetime$50
To find payback for any nCPA, you ask "when does cumulative CM cross that dollar amount?" — e.g. nCPA $15 → <1 mo, $25 → ~3 mo, $35 → ~9 mo, $50 → never. As cost creeps toward the ~$50 ceiling you need almost their whole 2-year life to break even, so the line goes near-vertical — and past ~$50 they never pay back (the line just stops).
How $50 is derived: mature 2024-and-earlier cohorts, contribution margin — cumulative contribution margin per new customer over 2 years (net revenue − COGS − shipping cost — the same lean definition as the Monthly KPI dashboard; ad spend is the separate CAC, not subtracted here). Same lean CM definition as the Monthly KPI, the cohort grid and the 2-Year LTV page. It's a blend — ~2.2 orders & ~4% subscribers on average, so a loyal repeat-buyer is worth more and a one-and-done less. For a scaling call the blended average is the right number: the next ad dollar buys that whole mix.

New vs. returning — why early payback is slow. A customer's first order is their least profitable one: a new customer's first order yields only ~$18 contribution margin vs ~$26 for a returning order (mostly smaller starter baskets, not a different margin rate). So the margin that pays back your nCPA leans on the repeat tail, not order one. That sets the reading line: nCPA below the $18 first-order line is cash-positive on order one (recouped before they ever reorder); above it, you're floating the cost until they come back — the further above, the more reorders you're banking on.

Plan caveats
Plan caveats. The spend floor is the budgeted monthly figure from the 2026 forecast; the +25% upside is a deliberate lean-in, gated by the live new-customer-cost light, not a fitted response curve (a monthly spend↔new regression is too noisy — spend & demand co-move with promos/season). Expected new customers and payback assume efficiency holds as spend rises toward the upside; if nCPA climbs, take less of it. Payback comes from the LTV→GP curve at the expected cost/customer. Proj. net rev is spend-responsive: new-customer revenue (the new customers this spend buys × ~$41 net per first order) + a seasonal returning-customer base (prior-year same-month repeat revenue × 4% YoY). So flexing spend up lifts the new-revenue half; the returning base is locked in from prior cohorts. MER = proj net ÷ spend. Execution (Meta/Google split, campaign structure) sits with the agency.

Scope. The spend plan was first recorded 2026-06-24, so Jul 2026 is the earliest month with a before-the-month projection — there is no earlier plan to score. For the month in progress, the plan's spend, customers and revenue are pro-rated by days elapsed (an estimate of pace, not the plan's own number); cost per customer is a rate and is compared directly. Promos are marked for context. What the misses teach us. The biggest miss in the record is the Feb–Jul 2024 spend push: $181,658 of ads, which a straight-line plan (every extra dollar buys customers at the recent average price) said would bring 12,378 new customers. It brought 7,991 (-35%). Extra spend buys customers at a rising price, so the 2027 plan below models diminishing returns instead of a straight line. Revenue misses are much smaller than customer misses because most revenue comes from returning customers, which spend barely moves.
How it's scored
The recorded plans only go back to June 2026, so this replays the same maths over every month since Jan 2024, each time using only the data that existed before that month. Two tests:

1. The 6-month spend plan (the Forward Spend Plan above). Each month is given the ad spend we actually made — spend is a decision, so the fair question is "told the spend, how close was it?" — and predicts new customers at the trailing-6-month cost per customer, plus returning-customer revenue at last year's level +4%.

2. The year-ahead method used for 2027. Each month = the same month last year, adjusted for the past year's trend in returning-customer revenue, order size and new-customer volume, with diminishing returns on any change in spend. Tested exactly as we're using it now: a plan made at the end of September for the next 12 months, in 2024 and in 2025.

How to read this
Four spend levels for 2027, each as a % change on 2026's ad spend ($348,344: Jan–Sep actual, the rest at budget), keeping 2026's month-by-month shape. The key column is cost of the extra customers: what each step up buys, divided into what it costs. The average cost per customer always looks fine because it blends in the cheap customers we'd get anyway; the extra ones are the ones the extra dollars actually buy. If they cost more than the $50 a customer is worth over two years, that step loses money even though the average looks healthy. Profit after ads = new customers × $50 two-year contribution margin − ad spend: the step with the highest number is the one that earns the most (an estimate).

Honesty notes
Honesty notes. (1) Weekly nCPA is noisy (promo timing, seasonality, day-mix) — read the trend, not single-week jumps. (2) Break-even $50 is the full blended lifetime contribution margin per new customer (mature 2024-and-earlier cohorts, contribution margin); it splits hard by first-order type — a first-order subscriber is worth ~$118 (5.4 orders / 81% repeat over 2yr) vs ~$47 for a one-time first order. The blend is the right break-even for a scaling call (the next ad dollar buys that mix), but every subscriber you convert is worth far more. (3) The first order is a new customer's least profitable (~$18 CM vs ~$26 for a repeat order), so nCPA below ~$18 is cash-positive on the very first order; above that you recoup it over the repeat tail — which is what the payback figure measures.
